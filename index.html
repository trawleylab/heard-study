The HEARD Study: Healthcare Equity, Access, and Research in Diabetes – A National Survey of Community Priorities

Steven L Trawley1 · Amanda Stephens2 · Clare Wilson1 · Grant Brinkworth3

1 The Cairnmillar Institute, Melbourne, VIC
2 Monash University, Faculty of Medicine, Nursing and Health Sciences, Melbourne, VIC
3 Diabetes Australia, Glebe, NSW

Asked what researchers should work on, Australians living with diabetes put affording care first.

And what they name as the top priority depends on how secure their finances are.

Download the A0 poster (PDF) 841 × 1189 mm, live text throughout · about 170 KB

What we did

  • A national cross-sectional online survey of 981 Australian adults (aged 18+) with self-reported diabetes, run from 13 August to 28 September 2025.
  • Recruited nationally through a commercial research panel (70%) and paid social media advertising (30%).
  • Priorities were elicited with a single open-text question, with no prompts and no pre-set categories: “Besides finding a cure, what do you think researchers should focus on to help people with diabetes live well every day?”
  • Responses were coded inductively by a locally hosted large language model, audited blind by a second model from a different family (78.2% primary-theme agreement), then reviewed by three authors and adjudicated by the lead author. All inference ran offline on local hardware; no participant text was sent to any external service.
  • 125 responses (12.7%) contained no codeable research priority and were excluded, leaving 856 for analysis.
  • Financial wellbeing was measured with the five-item CFPB Financial Well-Being Scale (0–100) and split at the scale midpoint of 50.

What we found

Six research priority themes were identified. Healthcare access, affordability and equity led, named by 28.5% of participants (n = 244 of 856; 95% CI 25.6–31.6).

Each response was assigned to one primary theme, so percentages sum to about 100%.

Within that leading theme, 65.2% of responses (159 of 244) concerned the cost and subsidisation of diabetes technology.

Financial wellbeing changed what came first

Diabetes group and financial wellbeing% naming access
Insulin-deficient, financially vulnerable34.5
Insulin-resistant, financially vulnerable31.8
Insulin-resistant, financially secure25.8
Insulin-deficient, financially secure20.5

For financially secure participants with insulin-deficient diabetes, technology led instead (28.2%). Across the four subgroups χ² = 112, df = 15, p < 0.001. The gradient held across tertiles of financial wellbeing (34.4% in the lowest to 23.1% in the highest, p = 0.002) and after weighting to NDSS population benchmarks for diabetes type and sex (27.1%).

In their words

“I’d LOVE a pump, it would solve all my diabetes issues but being in financial hardship means I won’t ever be able to have one” Type 1 diabetes, financially vulnerable
“Long lasting insulin pumps without having to count carbs” Type 1 diabetes, financially secure

What it means

  • Healthcare access, affordability and equity was the dominant research priority — but financial wellbeing, not diabetes type alone, shaped which priorities emerged.
  • Research agendas and funding should address structural barriers to care alongside biomedical advances.
  • Priority-setting exercises that report findings by diabetes type alone risk overlooking the needs of those most affected.

Who took part

Mean age 54.4 (SD 15.0) years; 66% women; 72% type 2 and 25% type 1 diabetes; 39% using continuous glucose monitoring. Mean CFPB financial wellbeing 48.6 (SD 14.7).
